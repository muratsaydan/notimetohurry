Remembering How to Breathe in a World Doing a Billion Operations a Second

The other day I joined an AI training online. I've been part of a community for two years now; whenever a new session opens, I'm there. It's been three or four years since AI entered our lives, and from day one I've followed every development without missing a single one.

Let me confess: in the early days I turned my nose up at this technology. "It's a toy," I said. That toy, the other day, laid out in seconds the things I'd spent years learning.

As the screen's light hit my face, I slumped into the chair. Let me sum up in one word what I felt down to my bones: inadequacy.

Here's the funny part: I'm someone who makes a living from technology. The number of people following these developments as closely as I do is probably less than one in a thousand worldwide. Statistically I ought to feel like some kind of pioneer, right?

Quite the opposite. In the middle of that training I felt like a dinosaur, out of breath, in a footrace against fibre-optic cables. The machine updates with a new version every week; and because I can't keep up with that pace, I'm literally ashamed of my own biology. Can you imagine?

In that "throwing an error" moment, I understood once more why I've been thinking so hard about this business of slowing down.

Our biggest mistake is this: we try to sync our heartbeat to the processor's clock speed. But the human brain has no capacity to keep pace, at the same speed, with a technology that changes every fraction of a second. We're made of flesh, bone, and — unfortunately — a nervous system with limits.

The machine can speed up. That's its nature; let it. The problem isn't the machine's speed, but our mistaking that speed for a measure of success and imposing it on ourselves.

Who's the master of the tool?

A thinker named Ivan Illich splits tools into two kinds. Some strengthen us: like a bicycle, like a pen — you decide what to do. Others grow and grow until, past a point, you begin to serve them; and when you don't use them, you fall out of life itself.

Which one is AI? It can be either. What makes the difference isn't the tool itself, but whose hand is holding it. I'll go on using these tools to the hilt for my work. But with one difference: the moment the training ends and I close the laptop lid, I want to be able to leave that frantic speed on the desk.

However fast you run on a treadmill, the place you arrive is always the same: right where you are.

This site isn't called the Nowhere-to-Hurry club for nothing. At some point I realised the point isn't keeping up — it's being able to tell which speed is mine and which belongs to the machine.

Technology will keep doing billions of operations a second. I'll be its user, not its captive. The machine's speed belongs to the machine; my biological speed belongs to me. Even in the most chaotic hour of the day, pressing "pause" by my own will and simply stopping — optimising nothing, processing nothing — that's the whole point.

Because no algorithm has yet managed to code that deep calm of looking at the sky and doing nothing. And that, I suppose, is one of the most human things we have left.
